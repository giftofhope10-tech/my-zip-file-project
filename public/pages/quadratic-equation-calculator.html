What Is a Quadratic Equation?

A quadratic equation is a second-degree polynomial equation in one variable, written in the standard form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a, b and c are real numbers and a ≠ 0. The highest power of the variable is 2, which is what makes it "quadratic" (from the Latin quadratus, meaning "square").

The solutions to a quadratic equation are called roots. A quadratic equation always has exactly two roots (counting multiplicity), which can be two distinct real numbers, one repeated real number, or a pair of complex conjugate numbers. The nature of the roots is determined by the discriminant, Δ = b² − 4ac.

How the Calculator Works

When you enter the three coefficients and press Solve, the calculator performs the following steps:

  1. Validation — it checks that all three coefficients are valid numbers and that a is not zero (if a were zero, the equation would be linear, not quadratic).
  2. Discriminant calculation — it computes Δ = b² − 4ac and uses the sign of the discriminant to determine the nature of the roots.
  3. Root calculation — it applies the quadratic formula: x = (−b ± √Δ) / (2a).
  4. Classification — if Δ > 0, there are two distinct real roots. If Δ = 0, there is one repeated real root. If Δ < 0, the square root of a negative number produces a complex result, and the calculator returns the two complex conjugate roots.
  5. Formatting — results are displayed with the full substitution and calculation shown step by step.

The Quadratic Formula

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a
Δ = b² − 4ac
a — coefficient of x² (must not be 0) b — coefficient of x c — constant term Δ — discriminant

The sign of the discriminant tells us everything about the roots before we even compute them:

  • Δ > 0: Two distinct real roots. The parabola crosses the x-axis at two points.
  • Δ = 0: One repeated real root. The parabola touches the x-axis at exactly one point (its vertex).
  • Δ < 0: Two complex conjugate roots. The parabola does not cross the x-axis.

Step-by-Step Examples

Example 1: Two real roots — x² − 5x + 6 = 0

Input: a = 1, b = −5, c = 6
Discriminant: Δ = (−5)² − 4(1)(6) = 25 − 24 = 1
Since Δ > 0: Two distinct real roots
Apply formula: x = (5 ± √1) / 2 = (5 ± 1) / 2
Root 1: x = (5 + 1) / 2 = 3
Root 2: x = (5 − 1) / 2 = 2
Result: x = 3 or x = 2

Example 2: Complex roots — x² + 2x + 5 = 0

Input: a = 1, b = 2, c = 5
Discriminant: Δ = 2² − 4(1)(5) = 4 − 20 = −16
Since Δ < 0: Two complex conjugate roots
Apply formula: x = (−2 ± √(−16)) / 2 = (−2 ± 4i) / 2
Root 1: x = −1 + 2i
Root 2: x = −1 − 2i

History of Quadratic Equations

The study of quadratic equations spans thousands of years. The earliest known solutions date to ancient Babylon (around 2000 BCE), where clay tablets show methods for solving problems equivalent to quadratic equations. Babylonian mathematicians used a geometric approach, completing the square literally — by rearranging shapes — rather than using algebraic notation.

The Greek mathematician Euclid described a geometric method for solving quadratics in his Elements (around 300 BCE). The Indian mathematician Brahmagupta (628 CE) was the first to give an explicit general formula for solving quadratic equations, recognising that a could be negative and that two roots might exist.

The word "algebra" itself comes from the title of a book by the Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi, Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing", c. 820 CE). Al-Khwarizmi's methods for solving quadratic equations were systematic and complete, covering all cases with positive roots. His work was translated into Latin in the 12th century and became the foundation of European algebra.

The modern quadratic formula, which works for all cases including negative and complex roots, was made possible by the acceptance of negative numbers and complex numbers in the 16th and 17th centuries. The Italian mathematician Girolamo Cardano published methods for solving cubic and quartic equations in 1545, which implicitly required understanding complex roots of quadratics.

Where Quadratic Equations Are Used

  • Physics: Projectile motion — the height of a thrown object follows a quadratic equation in time.
  • Engineering: Beam deflection, antenna design and signal processing.
  • Economics: Profit optimisation, where revenue and cost functions intersect quadratically.
  • Computer graphics: Ray tracing and intersection calculations with curved surfaces.
  • Finance: Break-even analysis and quadratic models for option pricing.

Advantages

  • The quadratic formula always works — it handles every case, including complex roots.
  • The discriminant tells you the nature of the roots before computing them.
  • Quadratic models fit many real-world phenomena that have a single maximum or minimum.

Limitations

  • Only applies to second-degree equations. Cubic, quartic and higher equations require different methods.
  • Floating-point arithmetic can introduce small rounding errors in the discriminant, especially when Δ is near zero.
  • The quadratic formula can suffer from catastrophic cancellation when subtracting two nearly equal numbers — for numerical stability, an alternative form is sometimes used in computing.
