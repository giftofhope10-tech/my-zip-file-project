What Is a Fraction?

A fraction represents a part of a whole. It consists of a numerator (the top number, which says how many parts you have) and a denominator (the bottom number, which says how many equal parts the whole is divided into). For example, 3/4 means three out of four equal parts.

A proper fraction has a numerator smaller than its denominator (like 3/4). An improper fraction has a numerator larger than or equal to its denominator (like 7/4). A mixed number combines a whole number and a proper fraction (like 1¾, which equals 7/4). Every improper fraction can be written as a mixed number and vice versa.

How the Fraction Calculator Works

When you enter two fractions and choose an operation, the calculator processes them in several stages:

  1. Parse — each input is read as an optional whole number, a numerator and a denominator. If a whole number is provided, the fraction is converted to an improper fraction first (e.g., 1¾ becomes 7/4).
  2. Validate — denominators are checked to ensure they are not zero. Numerators and denominators must be valid numbers.
  3. Operate — depending on the operation, the calculator either finds a common denominator (for addition and subtraction) or applies the multiply-across or invert-and-multiply rule (for multiplication and division).
  4. Simplify — the result is reduced to its lowest terms by dividing both numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor (GCD).
  5. Convert — if the result is an improper fraction, it is also shown as a mixed number and as a decimal.

Fraction Formulas

a/b + c/d = (a×d + c×b) / (b×d)
a/b − c/d = (a×d − c×b) / (b×d)
a/b × c/d = (a×c) / (b×d)
a/b ÷ c/d = (a×d) / (b×c)
a, c — numerators b, d — denominators

For addition and subtraction, the calculator finds a common denominator using the least common multiple (LCM) of the two denominators, which keeps the intermediate numbers as small as possible.

Step-by-Step Examples

Example 1: Adding 3/4 + 1/2

Input: 3/4 + 1/2
Common denominator: LCM(4, 2) = 4
Convert: 3/4 stays as 3/4; 1/2 becomes 2/4
Add: (3 + 2) / 4 = 5/4
Simplify: 5/4 = 1¼ (mixed number) = 1.25 (decimal)
Result: 5/4 or 1¼

Example 2: Multiplying 2/3 × 3/5

Input: 2/3 × 3/5
Multiply numerators: 2 × 3 = 6
Multiply denominators: 3 × 5 = 15
Result: 6/15
Simplify: GCD(6, 15) = 3, so 6/15 = 2/5
Final: 2/5 = 0.4

History of Fractions

Fractions are among the oldest mathematical concepts. The ancient Egyptians used unit fractions — fractions with a numerator of 1 — as early as 1800 BCE. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, dating to around 1550 BCE, contains tables for expressing fractions like 2/5 as sums of unit fractions (1/3 + 1/15). This system was practical for division of grain and land but cumbersome for general calculation.

The Babylonians took a different approach, using a sexagesimal (base-60) system that naturally represented fractions with denominators of 60, 3600 and so on. This is why we still divide hours into 60 minutes and minutes into 60 seconds.

The modern notation of a horizontal bar separating numerator and denominator was introduced by the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta in the 7th century and transmitted to Europe through Arabic mathematical texts. Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) helped popularise the Hindu-Arabic numeral system, including fractions, in Europe.

The word "fraction" comes from the Latin frangere, meaning "to break" — a fraction is literally a broken piece of a whole.

Where Fractions Are Used

  • Cooking: Recipes use fractional measurements (½ cup, ¼ teaspoon) that often need scaling.
  • Carpentry and construction: Measurements in inches are naturally fractional (3/8", 1/16").
  • Music: Time signatures (3/4, 6/8) and note durations are fractional.
  • Finance: Stock prices were historically quoted in fractions (e.g., 12½ dollars).
  • Education: Fractions are foundational for algebra, ratios, probability and proportional reasoning.

Advantages

  • Fractions give exact answers, unlike decimals which may need rounding.
  • They are natural for expressing ratios and proportions.
  • Working with fractions builds number sense and algebraic reasoning.

Limitations

  • Calculations with fractions can produce large intermediate numbers before simplification.
  • Not all fractions convert neatly to decimals (e.g., 1/3 = 0.333…).
  • Comparing fractions requires finding a common denominator or cross-multiplying.
