What Is Algebra?

Algebra is the branch of mathematics in which letters and symbols represent numbers and quantities in equations and formulas. Instead of working with known numbers alone, algebra lets you manipulate unknown quantities — typically represented by letters like x, y or z — to find their values. The core skill of algebra is solving equations: using balanced operations to isolate the unknown on one side of the equals sign.

A linear equation is one where the highest power of the variable is 1 (e.g., 3x + 7 = 22). A quadratic equation has a variable raised to the power 2 (e.g., x² − 5x + 6 = 0). Linear equations have exactly one solution; quadratic equations have two (which may be real, repeated or complex).

How the Calculator Works

For linear equations of the form ax + b = c, the calculator performs the same steps you would by hand:

  1. Subtract b from both sides: ax = c − b
  2. Divide by a: x = (c − b) / a
  3. Validate: if a = 0, the equation has no unique solution (either infinitely many or none, depending on c and b).

For quadratic equations, the calculator uses the standard quadratic formula and discriminant, the same method as the dedicated Quadratic Equation Calculator. The expression evaluator applies standard order of operations — parentheses first, then exponents, then multiplication and division (left to right), then addition and subtraction.

Algebraic Formulas

Linear: ax + b = c → x = (c − b) / a
Quadratic: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a
Distributive: a(b + c) = ab + ac
FOIL: (a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd
a, b, c — known coefficients x — unknown variable

Step-by-Step Examples

Example 1: Solving 3x + 7 = 22

Input: a = 3, b = 7, c = 22
Step 1: Subtract 7 from both sides: 3x = 22 − 7 = 15
Step 2: Divide both sides by 3: x = 15 / 3
Result: x = 5

Example 2: Evaluating 2(3 + 4)² − 10 / 2

Parentheses: (3 + 4) = 7
Exponent: 7² = 49
Multiply: 2 × 49 = 98
Divide: 10 / 2 = 5
Subtract: 98 − 5 = 93
Result: 93

History of Algebra

The word "algebra" comes from the Arabic al-jabr, a term used in the title of a book written around 820 CE by the Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi: Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing"). Al-Khwarizmi's work systematically presented methods for solving linear and quadratic equations. The terms al-jabr ("completion" — moving subtracted terms to the other side) and al-muqabala ("balancing" — subtracting the same quantity from both sides) describe two of the fundamental operations of algebra that we still perform today.

Al-Khwarizmi's name was Latinised as "Algoritmi," which is the origin of the English word algorithm. His book was translated into Latin in the 12th century and became the primary algebra textbook in European universities for centuries.

Before al-Khwarizmi, Greek mathematicians like Diophantus (c. 250 CE) had worked with equations, and Indian mathematicians like Brahmagupta (628 CE) had developed rules for solving quadratics. But al-Khwarizmi was the first to present algebra as a systematic, general discipline independent of specific problems.

The use of letters for unknown quantities was pioneered by François Viète in the late 16th century, and the familiar convention of using x for the unknown was popularised by René Descartes in his La Géométrie (1637).

Where Algebra Is Used

  • Education: Foundation for all higher mathematics, physics and engineering.
  • Computer science: Algorithm design, complexity analysis and cryptography.
  • Economics: Supply and demand models, equilibrium calculations and optimisation.
  • Engineering: Circuit analysis, structural calculations and control systems.
  • Everyday life: Budgeting, unit pricing and any "find the unknown" problem.

Advantages

  • Algebra provides a general framework for solving problems with unknown quantities.
  • Once an equation is set up, the solution process is mechanical and can be automated.
  • Algebraic manipulation skills transfer to calculus, physics and computer science.

Limitations

  • This calculator handles linear and quadratic equations; higher-degree polynomials require different methods.
  • The expression evaluator works with numbers, not symbolic variables — it cannot simplify 2x + 3x to 5x.
  • Simultaneous equations (systems with multiple variables) require matrix methods or substitution, not handled here.

Frequently Asked Questions

An expression is a combination of numbers and variables without an equals sign, like 2x + 3. An equation includes an equals sign and states that two expressions are equal, like 2x + 3 = 11. Expressions can be evaluated or simplified; equations can be solved for the unknown.
An equation is a balance — the two sides are equal. If you add, subtract, multiply or divide both sides by the same amount, the balance is preserved and the equation remains true. This principle is the foundation of all equation solving: you isolate the unknown by performing the inverse of each operation on both sides.
If a = 0, the equation becomes 0x + b = c, which means b = c. If b and c are actually equal, the equation is true for any x (infinitely many solutions). If b and c are not equal, there is no solution. Either way, the equation is no longer really about x.
PEMDAS stands for Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication, Division, Addition, Subtraction — the order in which operations should be performed. Without a standard order, an expression like 2 + 3 × 4 could be interpreted as either 20 or 14. PEMDAS ensures everyone gets the same answer: 14, because multiplication is performed before addition.
Not directly — this calculator handles one equation at a time. For systems of linear equations with multiple variables, the Matrix Calculator can solve them using matrix inversion. You can also solve simple systems by hand using substitution or elimination, then verify each step here.
Arithmetic works with specific numbers — addition, subtraction, multiplication and division of known values. Algebra generalises this by introducing variables (letters representing unknown or changing quantities) and the rules for manipulating them. Algebra lets you express general relationships and solve for unknowns, while arithmetic computes specific results.
