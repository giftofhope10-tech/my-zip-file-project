What Is a Matrix?

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers arranged in rows and columns. A matrix with m rows and n columns is called an m × n matrix (read "m by n"). Each number in the matrix is called an element or entry, identified by its row and column position (e.g., a₂₃ is the element in row 2, column 3).

Matrices are the central object of linear algebra. They are used to represent systems of linear equations, linear transformations (rotations, reflections, scalings in computer graphics), data in statistics, networks in graph theory, and quantum states in physics. Understanding matrix operations is essential for fields from machine learning to structural engineering.

How the Calculator Works

The calculator builds two matrices from your cell inputs, validates their dimensions for the selected operation, then computes the result:

  • Addition and subtraction — element-by-element; both matrices must have the same dimensions.
  • Multiplication — the number of columns in A must equal the number of rows in B. The result has dimensions (rows of A) × (columns of B).
  • Transpose — rows become columns and vice versa; operates on Matrix A only.
  • Determinant — only defined for square matrices; computed by cofactor expansion for 2×2 or 3×3.
  • Inverse — only defined for square matrices with non-zero determinant; computed using the adjugate and determinant.

Matrix Formulas and Rules

(A×B)ᵢⱼ = Σ(aᵢₖ × bₖⱼ) for k = 1 to n

History of Matrix Theory

Matrices were introduced as a formal mathematical object in the 19th century, though the ideas behind them are much older. Ancient Chinese mathematicians solved systems of linear equations using methods equivalent to Gaussian elimination as early as the Han dynasty (c. 200 BCE — 200 CE), as recorded in the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art.

The modern theory of matrices was developed independently by the British mathematicians Arthur Cayley and James Joseph Sylvester in the 1850s. Cayley's 1858 A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices established matrix algebra as a distinct branch of mathematics, defining matrix multiplication, the identity matrix, and the inverse. Sylvester coined the term "matrix" (from the Latin for "womb"), because he saw matrices as objects from which determinants could be "born."

The determinant, a scalar value derived from a square matrix, was studied earlier — by the Japanese mathematician Seki Takakazu in 1683 and the German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz in 1693, both independently. The determinant provides crucial information about a matrix: whether it has an inverse, whether a system of equations has a unique solution, and the scaling factor of the linear transformation the matrix represents.

Where Matrices Are Used

  • Computer graphics: 3D transformations — rotation, scaling, translation — are all matrix operations.
  • Machine learning: Neural networks are essentially chains of matrix multiplications.
  • Physics: Quantum mechanics uses matrices to represent observables and state transformations.
  • Economics: Input-output models and linear programming.
  • Engineering: Structural analysis, circuit theory and control systems all rely on matrix equations.

Advantages

  • Matrices compactly represent and manipulate large systems of equations.
  • Matrix operations are highly parallelisable, making them ideal for GPU computation.
  • The determinant and inverse provide essential information about solvability and stability.

Limitations

  • Matrix multiplication is not commutative: A × B ≠ B × A in general.
  • Not all matrices have inverses — only square matrices with non-zero determinant do.
  • Large matrices can be computationally expensive, especially for determinant and inverse.

Frequently Asked Questions

Matrix multiplication (A×B) combines rows of A with columns of B to produce a new matrix. Element-wise multiplication (the Hadamard product) multiplies corresponding entries: (A∘B)ᵢⱼ = aᵢⱼ × bᵢⱼ. Matrix multiplication is the standard operation in linear algebra; element-wise multiplication is used in specific applications like neural network backpropagation.
Each element of the product is computed by taking the dot product of a row of A with a column of B. A row of A has as many elements as A has columns, and a column of B has as many elements as B has rows. For the dot product to be defined, these must be equal — hence A's columns must equal B's rows.
A determinant of zero means the matrix is singular — it does not have an inverse. Geometrically, the linear transformation it represents collapses space into a lower dimension (for example, flattening a 2D area into a line or a point). In equation solving, a zero determinant means the system either has no solution or infinitely many solutions.
For a 2×2 matrix [a, b; c, d], the inverse is (1/det) × [d, −b; −c, a]. For larger matrices, the inverse is (1/det) × adjugate, where the adjugate is the transpose of the cofactor matrix. The calculator uses this method for 3×3 matrices. If the determinant is zero, no inverse exists.
No. In general, A × B ≠ B × A. The order matters because each row of the left matrix is paired with each column of the right matrix. This is why matrix multiplication is defined with a specific left-to-right order and why transformation sequences in computer graphics must be applied in the correct order.
The transpose of a matrix flips it over its diagonal, switching rows with columns. The element at position (i, j) moves to position (j, i). A 2×3 matrix becomes a 3×2 matrix when transposed. The transpose is used in many applications, including solving least-squares problems and defining symmetric and orthogonal matrices.
