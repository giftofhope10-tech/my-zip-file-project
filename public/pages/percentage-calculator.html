What Is a Percentage?

A percentage expresses a number as a fraction of 100. The word comes from the Latin per centum, meaning "by the hundred." When you say "15 per cent," you are saying 15 out of every 100 — or 15/100, which equals 0.15. Percentages provide a standardised way to compare proportions, changes and rates without being tied to any particular base unit.

Percentages are used everywhere: discounts in shops, interest rates on loans, tax rates, exam scores, survey results, battery levels and data dashboards. Understanding how to calculate them — and how they can mislead — is one of the most practically useful mathematical skills in everyday life.

Percentage Formulas

X% of Y = (X / 100) × Y
X is what % of Y = (X / Y) × 100
% Increase = ((New − Original) / Original) × 100
% Decrease = ((Original − New) / Original) × 100
% Difference = |A − B| / ((A + B) / 2) × 100
Original = Final / (1 ± P/100)
X, Y — input values P — percentage |A−B| — absolute difference

History of Percentage Calculations

The concept of expressing quantities as parts of a hundred dates back to ancient Rome, where taxes were often calculated as fractions of 100 (the centesima, or "hundredth part"). The Arabic mathematical tradition, which preserved and extended Greek and Indian mathematics, further developed proportional reasoning during the medieval period.

The familiar percent sign "%" evolved gradually. It originated as an abbreviation for the Italian per cento — "per hundred" — in the 15th century. Merchants and scribes wrote "p 100" or "p cento," and over time the abbreviation was contracted. By the 17th century, the modern "%" symbol, formed by the two zeros of "cento" separated by a slash, had become standard in European commercial documents.

Percentages became essential with the growth of banking, trade and taxation in Renaissance Italy and the Low Countries. Double-entry bookkeeping, described by Luca Pacioli in 1494, relied heavily on proportional calculations. The industrial revolution and the rise of statistics in the 19th century made percentages a universal language for data communication.

Where Percentages Are Used

  • Shopping: Discounts, sales tax, tips and price comparisons.
  • Finance: Interest rates, investment returns, loan APRs and inflation.
  • Education: Exam scores, grade boundaries and pass rates.
  • Statistics: Survey results, polling margins and demographic breakdowns.
  • Business: Profit margins, growth rates, market share and KPIs.

Advantages

  • Provides a standardised scale for comparison regardless of the original units.
  • Intuitive — most people understand "20% off" more readily than "0.2 reduction."
  • Essential for financial literacy and everyday consumer decisions.

Limitations

  • Percentages can be misleading without context — a "100% increase" sounds dramatic but means doubling, not multiplying by 100.
  • Percentage change is not symmetric: increasing from 50 to 100 is a 100% increase, but decreasing from 100 to 50 is only a 50% decrease.
  • Percentage difference uses the average as a base, which can produce unexpected results when the two values are very different in magnitude.
